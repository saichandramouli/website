Four regulators acted inside one week, in four different postures: the EU set operating rules, Abu Dhabi issued a supervisory notice, the United States
imposed licensing, and Ontario’s securities regulator published what an inspection had already found.

European Union
10–11 Nov
Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/2487, adopted 10 November and published in the Official Journal on 11
November, lays down common rules for the establishment, operation and supervision of the AI regulatory sandboxes
required by the AI Act. The Official Journal entry gives a date of effect of 1 December 2026 and records entry into force
as pending notification. The 2 August 2027 deadline for member states to run a national sandbox comes from the AI Act
timeline as amended, not from this instrument.
EUR-Lex, OJ L
2026/2487
UAE · ADGM
10 Nov
The Financial Services Regulatory Authority issued FCCP Notice No. 222 of 2026 on Artificial Intelligence and Cyber
Risk Management, pairing AI governance and cyber risk in a single supervisory instrument for firms in the financial free
zone. Our view: the structural choice is the notable part — the two are supervised together rather than separately.
FinReg-E
summary
Canada · Ontario
12 Nov
The Ontario Securities Commission published Staff Notice 33-763, setting out what its examination of registered firms
found about AI use and governance. It reports most firms early in adoption, with weaknesses in governance and training
and instances of possible “AI washing” — marketing claims about AI that firms could not substantiate. The OSC is a
provincial regulator; Canada has no single national securities regulator. Our view: findings from a completed
examination are a different thing from guidance — they show what a regulator actually found when it looked.
OSC release, 12
Nov
United States · BIS
15 Nov
The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule extending export licensing
requirements to remote access to advanced AI chips in data centres outside the United States that supply large
amounts of compute to companies headquartered in certain jurisdictions, and formally rescinding the earlier AI Diffusion
Rule. Our view: the practical question for any business is whether compute it rents abroad now falls inside a licensing
requirement.

FBI FLASH-20261113-001 · 13 NOV 2026 · TEN SECTORS
The FBI named ten sectors already targeted by an open-source AI intrusion framework, and
pointed at the systems companies watch least
On 13 November the FBI issued FLASH-20261113-001, “Threat Overview of
ARTEX Autonomous Penetration Testing Framework”. ARTEX is an open-
source, model-driven penetration-testing framework published on GitHub in
July 2026. The bureau says a variety of malicious actors have used it since at
least August 2026 to “automate multiple phases of cyber intrusions at
machine speed”, from reconnaissance through to data exfiltration.
The alert names ten targeted sectors: financial services, government,
technology, defence, semiconductors, energy, healthcare, manufacturing,
telecommunications and legal. It reports that some intrusions reached
privileged access in under three hours, and singles out partner and supplier
portals, mobile backends, sales-support systems, legacy services and third-
party integrations as the entry points it names. Incidents described in the
accounts linked below include attacks on financial institutions in South Korea
involving personal information of roughly 67,000 individuals, and a supply-
chain compromise affecting customers of a cloud backup product.
Open: Infosecurity Magazine (Kevin Poireault) · SecureWorld · SafeBreach. The FLASH itself
is distributed to recipients rather than published at a public URL; these are bylined accounts
of it. SafeBreach sells breach-simulation software and has a commercial interest in this
subject.
WHAT MAKES THIS A BOARD ITEM
The entry points the FBI names are not the systems most security
budgets defend. Partner portals, supplier integrations and sales-
support tools are typically owned by a business function, built by
someone else, and inventoried nowhere.
Our view: the three-hour figure changes the planning assumption
more than the framework does. A response process that assumes a
day or two of dwell time before privileged access is a process built
for a different attacker.
A separate count by Censys put publicly exposed ARTEX consoles
above 1,600 in early November. That is a count of exposed consoles,
not of breaches, and it is Censys's figure rather than the bureau's.
