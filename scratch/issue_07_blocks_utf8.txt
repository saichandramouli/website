  [34.0, 82.0, 523.0, 118.8] PREPRINT · 7 NOV 2026 · AGENT COST AND ACCURACY Preprint: adding agents to an enterprise-style workflow multiplied the cost and rarely improved the result
  [34.0, 124.6, 539.1, 143.3] The authors held the model, tasks and tools fixed and varied only the architecture, comparing four designs in eight arms across 13 enterprise-style standard operating procedures — a benchmark built to resemble the long, branching, tool-heavy workflows companies run. They report that every
  [34.0, 146.4, 558.6, 228.1] coordinated arm (multi-agent or skill-augmented) spends 1.4–4.7× the tokens and 2.6–5.8× the latency of a single function-calling agent “for gains that are usually small and rarely significant”, making the single agent, in their words, the efficient default. Quality of implementation mattered more than the design choice: a plausible multi-agent build underperformed the single agent, while a targeted redesign was the only arm to beat the baseline significantly, at 75.6% task success against 67.4% — though the authors also report that no architecture is statistically separable in aggregate, and that added structure only “plausibly” pays off on the most complex procedures, which they call a directional and not yet significant trend. Their stated conclusions are that no architecture is universally best — the right choice depends on the procedure — and that added architectural cost has to be earned by a gain in task success. Authors' own measurements; not peer reviewed. The paper does not name the model held fixed across the arms, so the multiples describe that one setup rather than a general rule.
  [34.0, 233.6, 559.0, 249.6] Nandi S, Arora H, Bai Y, Patel U, Shah C, Kwon H, Zhang W, Chen W, Li CR, Sconfienza A, Goyal A, Lee Y, Xiong M, Ramachandra P. “Agent Architecture Cost-Success Trade-offs: A Controlled Evaluation across Enterprise-Style SOPs.” arXiv preprint v1, 7 Nov 2026. arxiv.org/abs/2611.12953 · DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2611.12953 (arXiv-issued)

  [34.0, 82.0, 557.4, 115.5] UNITED STATES · 4 NOV 2026 · SUPERVISORY FRAMEWORK, NOT A RULE US bank regulator issues its first AI framework for examiners — and declines to treat AI as a risk apart
  [34.0, 121.6, 560.7, 213.8] The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which supervises national banks and federal savings associations, issued Bulletin 2026-54 and a supervisory framework for examiners evaluating banks' use of AI. Note the terminology before searching for it: the document calls the technology “super intelligence”, or SI. Examiners will apply it to SI in normal-course activities including credit decisioning, payments, fraud detection, customer service, operations, risk management and market activities, with particular attention to generative and agentic systems; it sets out 16 supervisory components across six pillars and is described as a living document. Two points matter for a board. Examiners are told not to treat SI as a separate and distinct risk category, and banks need not define a separate SI risk appetite — SI risk belongs inside existing operational, compliance and credit frameworks. And scrutiny follows what a system is permitted to do and the consequences if it fails, not how sophisticated the model is. Status: the framework sets no new supervisory expectations or enforceable standards; Reuters notes it is not legally binding in the way a rule is, though banks typically comply closely with such guidance.
