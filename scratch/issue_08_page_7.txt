A labour-market preprint finds the early effects of
AI in who is working, not how many are working
A revised preprint posted to arXiv on 10 November examines how labour
outcomes have changed across occupations as AI capability advanced from
chatbots to reasoning models and agents. The authors build an occupational
AI exposure score from task-level assessments against O*NET task data and
link it to US Current Population Survey records. In the revised version, part-
time work declined, weekly hours rose slightly, and the workforce in exposed
occupations became older and more educated — effects appearing in the
composition of the workforce rather than in the number of jobs.
The authors' own caveats do most of the work here. They state that they
“refrain from interpreting the estimated coefficients as causal effects”. They
warn that standard errors are likely understated, because the public-use
survey lacks the identifiers needed for design-based corrections, and advise
weighting magnitude and direction over statistical significance. The exposure
score measures potential capability, not actual adoption or substitution. They
name post-pandemic recovery, interest rates and trade policy as possible
confounders.
Open: arXiv:2507.08244 — Jacob Dominski and Yong Suk Lee, University of Notre Dame,
econ.GN. Preprint, not peer reviewed. Version note: arXiv's own rendered copies carry a v3
stamp dated 10 Nov 2026 and the findings above, while the listing page we retrieved shows
only v1 from July 2025, whose reported results differ — an earlier version associated higher
exposure with reduced employment. We report the revised version and flag the discrepancy
rather than resolving it.
OUR SUMMARY · WHY A CHRO SHOULD CARE
Most workforce planning for AI is expressed as a headcount number.
If the early effects really do land in composition — hours, age mix,
education mix, full-time share — then a headcount forecast can look
stable while the shape of the workforce underneath it changes
considerably.
That is a measurement question you can answer in your own data
this quarter, without waiting for the literature to settle: it is the one
claim here you do not have to take on trust.
Given the caveats above, treat this as a hypothesis worth testing
internally, not a finding to plan on.

Siemens Energy reported record quarterly revenue this week, with its chief executive saying he has “no doubt” more data centres will be built in the
United States, while noting that gas remains the main driver of generation. Our view: for most businesses this is a cost and siting line rather than a
sustainability one — power availability is becoming a constraint on where compute can be bought, and therefore on what it costs. A US federal electricity
forecast attributing record consumption partly to AI data centres was also reported this week. Its figures are held back until they can be cited to the
agency's own publication.
Open: CNBC on Siemens Energy, 11 Nov.
