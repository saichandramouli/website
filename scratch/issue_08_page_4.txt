AGENTIC COMMERCE · 11–13 NOV 2026 · UNITED STATES, GLOBAL
Within 48 hours, one airline locked unauthorised third-party AI agents out of its
checkout and OpenAI set out what merchants must change to accept agent payments
— a decision and a requirement, landing two days apart
On 11 November United Airlines said it would restrict “unauthorized, third-
party personal AI agents or assistants” from its website and app, and
indirectly through travel agencies and aggregators. The settled part is the
transaction: reports of its revised contract say an agent may not book, change
or service a ticket, or buy seats, upgrades or extras — and that this holds
even where the passenger supervises and approves every step. United has
said that a consumer granting an agent access does not by itself mean the
agent can transact. Accounts differ on whether searching and displaying fares
are also restricted: some report that search remains open, others that the
terms reach it, and we have not resolved it. Separately, its website terms
prohibit using its pricing, schedules and availability data to train or evaluate
machine learning models. United gave account security, unintended
transactions and regulatory concerns as its reasons.
On 13 November OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet, which lets agents
complete purchases with user approval, using tokenised cards. It was built
with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, and is limited at launch to
ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the United States aged 18 or over with eligible US-
issued cards. OpenAI says it does not hold customer funds. OpenAI also
published a playbook for merchants. It says that to accept payment from the
wallet, websites need to ensure agents are “not inadvertently blocked”, and
that payment and risk systems need to recognise legitimate purchases made
by agents. It points merchants at their CDN and firewall settings and at Web
Bot Auth, which lets an agent sign its requests.

OUR SUMMARY · WHY THIS IS THE WEEK'S LEAD
Both point at one question: is an AI agent at the checkout a
customer or an intruder? Only the airline has answered it. OpenAI
has not — it has set out what a merchant must change in order to
answer yes, which is a condition of doing this business, not a
decision of its own.
Where a business has not taken this decision explicitly, it is being
settled anyway — by bot-detection rules, CDN settings and fraud
scoring configured before agents could buy anything. That is a
commercial policy set by default, by systems nobody briefed.
The unglamorous question underneath it is liability: when an agent
buys on a customer's behalf and the charge is disputed, who is
refunded, and on whose authority.

ONE MEASURED FINDING, WITH ITS CONFLICT STATED
In research reported by PhocusWire, two named agent products navigated 176 scripted booking requests as far as the payment step, with no captchas or
login walls stopping them. The requests included airline bookings but were not limited to them. The research is Propellic's, a travel marketing agency selling
services in the market it tested; the figure is its own and has not been independently replicated. Treat it as an indication that existing defences do not reliably
tell agents apart, not a measure of your own estate.
